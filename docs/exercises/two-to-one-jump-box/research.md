# 2 to 1 Jump Box: research

Batch 1, exercise 4 of 10 (GitHub issue #18). Movement reference: GymVisual #420813 (see `reference.md`). Two-foot countermovement and takeoff, landing on one leg on top of a plyo box, then stand tall and step down.

How to read the tags: **[trial evidence]** means a measured result from a published study (often of a related task, not this exact drill). **[clinical opinion]** means professional guidance or our own coaching judgment. No study we found tests the 2 to 1 Jump Box itself, so every joint-angle norm below is borrowed from single-leg landing, single-leg hop or drop-jump research. Treat them as authoring targets, not exact values for this drill.

## Exercise card

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Type | Plyometric (lower-body power plus single-leg landing control). GymVisual's data sheet says "Aerobic"; we treat it as plyometric [clinical opinion]. |
| Body part | Legs (quadriceps, glutes, calves) |
| Secondary | Core, hips (frontal-plane control), shoulders (arm swing) |
| Equipment | Plyo box (a soft-sided foam box is the safer choice) plus body weight [clinical opinion] |
| Difficulty | Intermediate. Harder than a two-foot box jump because the landing is on one leg [clinical opinion]. |
| Mechanics | Compound |
| Plane | Sagittal (main movement), with a frontal-plane control demand on the landing knee and hip |
| Laterality | Bilateral takeoff, unilateral landing (alternate legs). Author the right leg and mirror it. |

### Muscles

No EMG study of this exact drill was found. The list is built from single-leg landing EMG (Pilanthananond 2023) plus standard plyometric anatomy. Evidence for the glute and core roles is indirect.

- **Target**
  - Quadriceps, landing leg (also both legs at takeoff). Single-leg jump landings showed higher rectus femoris and vasti activity than drop landings in early impact [trial evidence].
  - Gluteus maximus, both legs at takeoff and the landing leg on the catch [clinical opinion].
  - Calves (gastrocnemius, soleus): ankle push-off at takeoff and the first part of the catch [clinical opinion]. Medial gastrocnemius was recorded in the landing EMG study but did not differ between landing tasks [trial evidence].
- **Synergist**
  - Hamstrings: hip extension at takeoff and support for the knee on landing. They were recorded in the landing EMG study, with no task difference [trial evidence; role is clinical opinion].
  - Adductor magnus: hip extension at takeoff [clinical opinion].
  - Shoulder flexors (anterior deltoid): forward arm swing that adds to takeoff [clinical opinion].
  - Trail-leg hip flexors: hold the free leg up and behind/below the body on landing [clinical opinion].
- **Stabilizer**
  - Gluteus medius, landing side: keeps the pelvis level and resists the knee caving in [clinical opinion; Physiopedia ties poor control to knee internal rotation during jumping].
  - Core (obliques, deep abdominals, spinal extensors): keep the trunk steady over the single foot [clinical opinion].
  - Tibialis anterior and peroneus longus, landing ankle: ankle and foot control at contact. Both were recorded in the landing EMG study [trial evidence; role is clinical opinion].
  - Foot intrinsics: balance on one foot [clinical opinion].

### Joint-angle norms

| Measure | Norm or target | Evidence | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Countermovement depth | Self-selected dip averaged about 0.36 m center-of-mass drop in a maximal vertical jump. A shallow dip (about 0.27 m) cut jump height by about 8%. A deeper dip (about 0.47 m) gave no extra height. The reference shows a quarter squat. No verified study gives a knee angle for a box-jump dip, so author a quarter-to-half squat. | [trial evidence] for depth versus height (10 men, maximal CMJ, not box jumps); [clinical opinion] for the knee angle | McHugh 2024 |
| Knee flexion at initial contact, single-leg landing | About 9 to 10 degrees after a forward jump from a 30 cm box; about 10 to 13 degrees in other single-leg landing and hop studies. Single-leg landings touch down with less knee bend than double-leg landings (about 16 to 18 degrees). | [trial evidence] for related tasks | Li 2023; Song 2024; Baldazzi 2026 |
| Peak knee flexion, single-leg landing | About 46 degrees with a natural landing and about 50 degrees with a "soft" landing. Knee flexion range from contact to peak was about 40 to 46 degrees in healthy players. Landing on a box top is a shorter fall than landing on the floor, so the drill may need less (not verified) [clinical opinion]. Author about 45 to 50 degrees. | [trial evidence] for related tasks | Song 2024; Baldazzi 2026 |
| Hip flexion, single-leg landing | About 26 to 27 degrees in a single-leg lateral jump-landing (100 ms before contact and at peak force). For comparison, a bilateral drop vertical jump reached about 90 degrees peak hip flexion. Single-leg data are thin. Author a visible hip hinge ("hip back"), about 30 to 45 degrees. | [trial evidence] (thin, indirect); authoring target is [clinical opinion] | PMC5011586 study; Mancini 2022 |
| Frontal-plane knee control | 2D knee valgus norms for a unilateral step landing: about 5 to 12 degrees (women) and 1 to 9 degrees (men). Single-leg drop-jump FPPA in active men averaged about 12 degrees. 3D knee abduction at contact was about 4 to 5 degrees, with about 7 to 10 degrees more during the landing. The 2D test changes by about 7.5 to 9 degrees between days, so small differences mean little. Target: knee tracks over the second and third toes, with no visible inward collapse. | [trial evidence] for norms; target is [clinical opinion] | Herrington & Munro 2010; Llurda-Almuzara 2020; Baldazzi 2026; Munro 2012 |

Context for the frontal-plane target:

- A single-leg land showed more 2D valgus than a two-foot drop jump in female footballers and basketballers. The authors advise screening with one-leg tasks as well as two-leg tasks [trial evidence; Munro 2017].
- 2D FPPA only weakly to moderately predicted 3D knee valgus moments in landing tasks [trial evidence; Straub & Powers 2022]. The video angle is a rough guide only.
- Physiopedia links landing with little knee bend and more valgus to ACL injury risk [clinical opinion summarizing trial evidence].

### Landing load context

- Single-leg landings produced higher peak vertical force (about 4.3 to 4.6 times body weight) than double-leg landings after a forward jump from a 30 cm box [trial evidence; Li 2023].
- A single-leg jump landing reached about 4.9 times body weight, versus about 2.7 for a single-leg drop landing, with a faster rise to peak [trial evidence; Pilanthananond 2023].
- In a single-leg lateral jump-landing, more knee flexion just before contact went with lower peak force [trial evidence; PMC5011586 study].
- A "soft" landing instruction raised peak knee flexion and lowered peak force (3.3 vs 3.7 times body weight) in single-leg landings [trial evidence; Song 2024].
- In drop jumps, the soft landing gave the lowest force and stiff landings the highest. No landing style showed valgus [trial evidence; Guy-Cherry 2018].
- Landing force asymmetry between legs was larger than takeoff asymmetry (about 11 to 13% vs about 4%) [trial evidence; Cone & Lee 2021]. This supports training each landing leg on purpose.
- A box jump probably reduces landing force compared with jumping in place, because the athlete lands near the top of the jump. This is widely taught. We found no verified study that measured it [clinical opinion; evidence gap].

### Breathing

No study addresses breathing for this drill [clinical opinion throughout].

- Breathe in while standing tall and during the dip.
- Breathe out sharply on the takeoff.
- Hold a short brace through the catch.
- Breathe out as you stand up on the box, then breathe normally while stepping down and resetting.

Cadence: about 0.5 s dip, a fast takeoff, about 1 to 2 s stick on the box, then a slow step down and a 2 to 3 s reset.

### Prescription

- **Frequency:** 2 to 3 sessions per week, with 48 to 72 h recovery between plyometric sessions [clinical opinion; Physiopedia citing Dick].
- **Progression:** start at low intensity. Progress bilateral to unilateral, stable to unstable, simple to complex [clinical opinion; Physiopedia citing Davies et al.]. This drill sits after the two-foot box jump and before one-to-one jumps.
- **Volume:** count foot contacts per session [clinical opinion; Physiopedia]. Suggested dose: 2 to 4 sets of 3 to 5 reps per landing leg, full recovery (about 60 to 90 s) between sets, every rep at full quality [clinical opinion; our suggestion, not taken from a verified source].
- **Box height:** start low (about 15 to 30 cm), then raise it only while the landing stays quiet and the knee stays aligned [clinical opinion].
- **Program length:** basic plyometric blocks run about 4 to 6 weeks [clinical opinion; Physiopedia].

### Cues

1. **Setup:** "Stand tall, an arm's length from the box, feet hip-width."
2. **Dip:** "Hips back into a quarter squat, arms swing back."
3. **Takeoff:** "Drive both feet through the floor and swing the arms up."
4. **Land:** "Land softly on one foot, knee bent over your toes, hips back."
5. **Stick:** "Knee tracks over the second toe. Freeze for two counts, free leg off the box."
6. **Stand:** "Stand tall on the box, then step down. Don't jump down."
7. **Reset:** "Reset fully before the next rep. Switch landing legs each set."

Basis: the soft landing, bent knee and hip, and knee-over-toe cues follow Physiopedia's ACL prevention guidance and the landing studies above [clinical opinion with indirect trial support]. Stepping down is standard coaching to avoid an unneeded drop landing [clinical opinion].

### Common mistakes and fixes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Knee caves inward on the landing leg | Lower the box. Cue "knee over second toe" and "spread the floor". Build glute med strength with single-leg squats. [clinical opinion; valgus norms above are trial evidence] |
| Stiff, straight-legged landing | Cue "land quietly"; bend the knee and hip on contact. A soft landing lowered peak force in studies. [trial evidence for force; cue is clinical opinion] |
| Box too high: the athlete tucks the knees and lands in a deep squat | Pick a height where the landing knee bend stays near the norm (about 45 to 50 degrees). Height is not the goal. [clinical opinion] |
| Hips stay forward and the trunk falls back on landing | Cue "hips back, chest over knee", then hinge. [clinical opinion] |
| Free leg touches down or the athlete hops to recover balance | Lower the box, slow the stick, hold the landing two counts before standing. [clinical opinion] |
| Jumping back down off the box | Step down every rep; a jump down adds an unplanned drop landing. [clinical opinion] |
| Rushing reps or training tired | Fully reset between reps and stop the set when landings get louder or the knee drifts. Physiopedia notes fatigue degrades landing control. [clinical opinion] |

### Safety: who should modify or skip

- **Contraindications for plyometrics** (Physiopedia citing Davies et al.) [clinical opinion]: unconditioned athletes, unyielding surfaces, a history of stress-related injury, acute or sub-acute sprains, acute inflammation, post-operative status, and joint instability.
- **Strength prerequisites** (Physiopedia citing Dick) [clinical opinion]: adequate strength and near-full range of motion before plyometrics, plus balance and body control.
- **Regressions** [clinical opinion]:
  - Two-foot box jump (two-foot landing).
  - Single-leg balance and hop-and-stick on the floor.
  - A lower or soft box.
- **ACL reconstruction or knee pain:** clear the drill with a clinician first. After ACL reconstruction, the operated limb may land with less knee bend [trial evidence; Baldazzi 2026, small male-only sample]. One-leg landing drills belong to the later, return-to-sport stages of rehab [clinical opinion].
- **Pregnancy, osteoporosis, or balance disorders:** skip or substitute a low-impact option [clinical opinion].
- **Equipment:** use a stable, non-slip box on a non-slip floor; a soft-sided box reduces shin-scrape risk on a missed jump [clinical opinion].

## 1. Scientific studies

Evidence on this exact drill is absent. The studies below cover related tasks: single-leg landings, drop jumps and countermovement depth.

- [McHugh et al. 2024, Effect of countermovement depth on the neuromechanics of a vertical jump (Transl Sports Med)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11208864). Self-selected dip about 0.36 m. A shallow dip cut jump height about 8%; a deeper dip added nothing.
- [Li, Song, Jenkins & Dai 2023, Prelanding knee kinematics and landing kinetics in single-leg vs double-leg landings (J Appl Biomech)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10498700). Single-leg landings touched down at about 9 to 10 degrees knee flexion, with higher forces than double-leg landings.
- [Song et al. 2024, Falling decreased ACL loading variables during single-leg landings after trunk perturbation (J Electromyogr Kinesiol)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10497447). Peak knee flexion about 46 degrees natural and 50 degrees soft; a soft landing lowered peak force.
- [Baldazzi et al. 2026, Knee angles at initial contact and post-landing knee ROM with and without ACL reconstruction (Sci Rep)](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/63f8bb46-45d4-4847-9678-c80b5939f226/2026_Baldazzi_SciRep_Knee%20angles.pdf). Single-leg hop and cross drop landing angles; the reconstructed limb landed with less knee bend.
- [Pilanthananond et al. 2023, Muscle activation and GRF in single-leg drop vs jump landing (Sports, MDPI)](https://mdpi.com/2075-4663/11/9/185). Jump landing had higher early quadriceps EMG and about 4.9 vs 2.7 times body weight peak force.
- [Single-leg lateral jump-landing in male collegiate athletes (PMC5011586)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5011586). Knee flexion about 41 degrees and hip flexion about 26 to 27 degrees; more pre-contact knee bend went with lower force. (Title and authors were not in the fetched text.)
- [Mancini et al. 2022, Effects of a soccer-specific vertical jump on landing kinematics (Sports Med Health Sci)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9453686). Bilateral drop vertical jump: peak knee flexion about 96 degrees and hip about 90 degrees (n = 8).
- [Herrington & Munro 2010, Drop jump landing knee valgus angle: normative data (Phys Ther Sport)](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1452441/drop-jump-landing-knee-valgus-angle-normative-data-in-a-physically-active-population). Step-landing 2D valgus: 5 to 12 degrees in women, 1 to 9 degrees in men (n = 100).
- [Munro, Herrington & Carolan 2012, Reliability of 2D video assessment of frontal-plane knee valgus (J Sport Rehabil)](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1437145/reliability-of-two-dimensional-video-assessment-of-frontal-plane-dynamic-knee-valgus-during-common-athletic-screening-tasks). Smallest detectable difference about 7.5 to 9 degrees; women had higher FPPA.
- [Munro, Herrington & Comfort 2017, 2D knee valgus in single-leg squat, single-leg land and drop jump (J Sport Rehabil)](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1394901/the-relationship-between-2d-knee-valgus-angle-during-single-leg-squat-single-leg-land-and-drop-jump-screening-tests). Single-leg land showed more valgus than the drop jump; screen with one-leg tasks.
- [Llurda-Almuzara et al. 2020, Normative data for dynamic knee valgus in healthy active males (Sci Rep)](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-020-74177-8). Single-leg drop jump from 50 cm: FPPA about 12 degrees (right leg).
- [Straub & Powers 2022, Does 2D FPPA predict frontal-plane knee moments? (IJSPT)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9718689). FPPA predicted 3D knee moments only weakly to moderately.
- [Guy-Cherry et al. 2018, Landing styles influence reactive strength index without increasing injury risk (Sports Med Int Open)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30539115/). Soft landing gave the lowest force (about 1.3 times body weight) and stiff the highest (1.8).
- [Cone & Lee 2021, Lower-limb force asymmetries during landing and jumping (Int J Exerc Sci)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8136551). Landing asymmetry (about 11 to 13%) exceeded takeoff asymmetry (about 4%).
- [Rojano Ortega et al. 2010, Vertical GRF in the landing phase of a countermovement jump (J Sports Sci Med)](https://jssm.org/jssm-09-282.xml-Fulltext). Floor landings after a CMJ peaked near 10 times body weight. This is useful context for why landing on a box top is gentler.
- [Leissring et al., Ground and knee joint reaction forces in plyometric exercises (ISBS Proceedings Archive; year not shown on page)](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/4420/4110). Single-leg jump about 3.0 vs CMJ about 2.0 times body weight peak force.

## 2. Clinical and professional articles
