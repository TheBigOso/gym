# 3/4 Sit-up: research

Batch 1, exercise 5 of 10 (GitHub issue #23). Movement reference: GymVisual #247613 (see `reference.md`). Researched 2026-11-26.

Evidence tags: **[trial evidence]** = measured in a study (EMG, modelling, randomized trial); **[clinical opinion]** = professional guidance, expert reasoning or consensus without direct trial data for this exact exercise.

Important caveat up front: no study found here tests a "3/4 sit-up" as a named variant. The research compares the full bent-knee sit-up with the crunch / curl-up (head and shoulders only). A 3/4 sit-up sits between them, so its numbers below are inferred from those two ends, not measured.

## Exercise card

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Type | Strength (dynamic trunk flexion) |
| Body part | Waist / core (abdominals) |
| Equipment | Body weight; exercise mat optional |
| Difficulty | Beginner to intermediate (harder than a crunch, easier than a full sit-up) |
| Mechanics | Compound in practice: spinal flexion, then hip flexion |
| Plane | Sagittal |
| Laterality | Bilateral, symmetric |

### Muscles

- **Target:** rectus abdominis. It is the main mover in every sit-up and crunch EMG study reviewed; in a 2016 study, upper rectus averaged about 40% MVIC in the bent-knee sit-up and 53% in the crunch. [trial evidence] (Escamilla et al. 2016)
- **Synergists:** external and internal obliques (about 36% and 31% MVIC in the bent-knee sit-up in the same study). [trial evidence] Hip flexors: iliopsoas and rectus femoris. All sit-up forms activated psoas more than the curl-up (about 15-35% MVC vs about 10%). [trial evidence] (Juker et al. 1998). Rectus femoris was about 23% MVIC in the bent-knee sit-up vs about 6% in the crunch. [trial evidence] (Escamilla et al. 2016)
- **Stabilizers:** transversus abdominis and the deep core (brace), with neck flexors holding the head. Lumbar paraspinals and latissimus stay low (about 5-8% MVIC). [trial evidence for the paraspinal / lat values; clinical opinion for the TrA role]
- **Hip flexors past the curl phase:** the commonly taught model is that the abdominals curl the trunk until the shoulder blades clear the floor, after which the hip flexors lift the curled trunk toward the thighs. [clinical opinion] Supporting data: lumbar flexion is about 3 degrees in a crunch vs about 30 degrees in a bent-knee sit-up, and hip-flexor EMG rises sharply in sit-ups. [trial evidence, cited by Escamilla et al. 2016 from Halpern and Bleck 1979] The exact trunk angle (often quoted as about 30 degrees) at which hip flexors "take over" was **not** confirmed in any source fetched here, so treat it as a teaching approximation. For a 3/4 sit-up that rises to about 60-70 degrees, the upper half of the rep is therefore mainly hip-flexor work with the abdominals holding the trunk curled. [clinical opinion]
- **Knee bend and the hip flexors:** bending the knees does not switch the hip flexors off. McGill's review reports bent-knee sit-ups produced *more* psoas activity than straight-leg ones, and pressing the heels down did not remove psoas activity. [trial evidence] (McGill 1998; Juker et al. 1998 as summarized by Perle 2002)

### Angle norms

| Angle | Norm | Source and strength |
|---|---|---|
| Knee | About 90 degrees, feet flat, hip-width | Matches the GymVisual reference and standard bent-knee sit-up setups. [clinical opinion] None of the fetched EMG papers reported a knee angle in its methods. |
| Hip (start) | About 45 degrees flexion (follows from feet flat, knees at 90) | [clinical opinion] |
| Trunk end angle ("3/4") | About 60-70 degrees from the floor (three-quarters of the way to upright) | GymVisual reference only. No research defines the 3/4 end point. [clinical opinion] |
| Curl-up / crunch end point, for comparison | Lower tips of the shoulder blades just off the floor | Used as the crunch end point in Youdas et al. 2008 [trial evidence] and in manual muscle testing (Physiopedia, rectus abdominis). |
| Lumbar flexion | About 3 degrees in a crunch vs about 30 degrees in a bent-knee sit-up | [trial evidence], Halpern and Bleck 1979 as cited by Escamilla et al. 2016 |

### Breathing

Exhale through the mouth as you curl up; inhale as you lower under control. Do not hold the breath. [clinical opinion; no trial compares breathing patterns for this exercise] A moderate brace raises intra-abdominal pressure, which some models predict lowers spinal compression by about 18%. [trial evidence from modelling, as cited by Contreras and Schoenfeld 2011; not measured in sit-ups]

### Prescription

| Item | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Sets x reps | 2-3 x 8-15; start untrained users at about 2 x 15 or fewer |
| Session cap | About 60 total spinal-flexion reps per session, across all flexion exercises |
| Frequency | 48 hours or more between dynamic flexion sessions (72 hours may be more prudent) |
| Tempo | About 2 s per rep, controlled; a lab protocol used 1 s up, 1 s hold, 1 s down |
| Timing | Avoid in the first hour after waking; walk a few minutes after long sitting first |
| Progression | Crunch / curl-up, then 3/4 sit-up, then full sit-up or added load; avoid ultra-high-rep sets |

All prescription values are [clinical opinion] (Contreras and Schoenfeld 2011; Ross, ACE 2011). The "about 60 reps" and morning-avoidance limits are expert reasoning extrapolated from cadaver data, not tested thresholds. The 1-1-1 tempo is from the Escamilla et al. 2016 protocol [trial evidence for the protocol, not for its effect].

### Cues

1. Lie on your back, knees bent about 90 degrees, feet flat and hip-width. Fingertips lightly behind the head, elbows wide.
2. Brace lightly and breathe out as you start: chin slightly tucked, curl the head and shoulders up first.
3. Peel the spine up one segment at a time; keep the ribs moving toward the hips.
4. Rise until the trunk is about three-quarters of the way up (about 60-70 degrees), not all the way to vertical.
5. Keep the feet down and the elbows wide; the hands only cradle the head.
6. Lower slowly with control, back and then head returning to the mat.
7. Reset briefly between reps; no bouncing off the floor.

All cues are [clinical opinion].

### Common mistakes and fixes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Pulling on the head with the hands (neck strain) | Fingertips only, elbows wide; or cross the arms on the chest. [clinical opinion] |
| Jerking up with momentum, then flopping down | 2 s up, controlled lowering; reduce range or regress to a crunch if needed. [clinical opinion] |
| Feet lifting or sliding | Reduce range; regress rather than anchor the feet. Anchoring the feet lets the hip flexors pull harder. [clinical opinion] |
| Heaving up with a flat, stiff back (hip flexors doing the lift) | Curl the head and shoulders first; stop at the 3/4 point. Hip flexion without trunk curl is cited as the main shear risk. [clinical opinion] (IDEA Fit) |
| Going all the way to vertical or past it | Stop at about 60-70 degrees; the top of the rep adds hip-flexor work and spine load without extra abdominal benefit. [clinical opinion] |
| Holding the breath | Exhale on the way up, inhale on the way down. [clinical opinion] |
| Too many reps (100+ per set) | Cap total flexion reps per session; add difficulty or load instead. [clinical opinion] |

### Safety: who should modify

- **Low-back pain, disc herniation or flexion intolerance:** avoid or swap for a McGill-style curl-up, dead bug or plank. [clinical opinion] Sit-ups put more than 3,000 N of compression on the lumbar spine (about 3,230 N straight-leg, 3,410 N bent-knee). [trial evidence, model estimates in 12 young men] (McGill 1995; McGill 1998) A basic crunch is about 2,000 N. [trial evidence, as cited by Contreras and Schoenfeld 2011 from Axler and McGill 1997] Rectus femoris activity in the bent-knee sit-up "may be problematic" for some people with low-back problems. [trial evidence for the EMG; clinical opinion for the risk] (Escamilla et al. 2006)
- **Osteoporosis or vertebral fracture:** avoid. Bone-health guidance says to avoid repeated or end-range spinal flexion, and lists sit-ups specifically. [clinical opinion] (Healthy Bones Australia 2024; Physiopedia, osteoporosis)
- **Pregnancy and postpartum (diastasis recti):** avoid sit-ups while rebuilding deep-core control; progress to superficial-abdominal work later. [clinical opinion, Delphi consensus as summarized by Physiopedia] Pre-activating transversus abdominis during a curl-up reduced linea alba distortion. [trial evidence, observational] (Lee and Hodges 2016, via Physiopedia) A supervised postpartum training class did not reduce diastasis prevalence at 6 months. [trial evidence] (Gluppe et al. 2018, via Physiopedia)
- **Neck pain:** support the head with the hands without pulling, or use a towel under the head; regress range. [clinical opinion]

### The low-back loading debate (stated fairly)

- **Case for caution (McGill and colleagues):** sit-ups load the lumbar spine above 3,000 N whether the knees are bent or straight, with "no biologically significant difference" between techniques. [trial evidence, model] (McGill 1995) Partial curl-ups had the best ratio of abdominal challenge to spine compression of 12 exercises tested. [trial evidence] (Axler and McGill 1997) Pig-spine studies produced disc herniations under thousands of repeated flexion cycles with compression. [trial evidence, in vitro animal model] McGill recommends curl-ups over sit-ups. [clinical opinion] (McGill, ACE 2003; Kravitz summary)
- **Counter-view (Contreras and Schoenfeld; Ross):** the herniation data come from cadaveric pig spines, flexed continuously for thousands of cycles, with no muscle or tissue remodelling. Living discs adapt to progressive loading, and no study has tested flexion exercise injury in living humans. [clinical opinion, based on a narrative review] They support moderate-volume flexion work for healthy people.
- **Field data:** in a cluster randomized trial of 1,141 soldiers, a sit-up-based program and a core stabilization program produced the same overall injury rates. [trial evidence] (Childs et al. 2010) Low-back restriction days were higher with sit-ups (8.3 vs 4.2 days), but the authors called this evidence weak. [trial evidence, marginal] In 2,616 soldiers, core training did not reduce sit-up test performance. [trial evidence] (Childs et al. 2009)
- **Bottom line:** the high compression of sit-ups is well measured. Whether that causes injury in healthy trainees at normal volumes is **contested and not settled**. People with existing back, bone or abdominal-wall issues are the clear group for whom modifying is advised. [clinical opinion]

## 1. Scientific studies

### Sit-up vs curl-up EMG

- [Escamilla et al. 2016, supine, prone and side exercises with and without a Swiss ball (Sports Health, PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4922527). Bent-knee sit-up vs crunch EMG; sit-up rectus femoris about 23% vs 6% MVIC; cites 30 vs 3 degrees lumbar flexion.
- [Escamilla et al. 2006, traditional and nontraditional abdominal exercises (Phys Ther; CSU Sacramento record)](https://scholars.csus.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Electromyographic-Analysis-of-Traditional-and-Nontraditional/99257847129401671). 21 adults; bent-knee sit-up had relatively high rectus femoris activity.
- [Juker, McGill et al. 1998, intramuscular psoas and abdominal EMG (Med Sci Sports Exerc; Neuchatel repository)](https://libra.unine.ch/handle/20.500.14713/50967). All sit-up forms activated psoas (15-35% MVC) more than the curl-up.
- [Youdas et al. 2008, Ab-Slide, crunch, double leg thrust and side bridge EMG (J Strength Cond Res; PDF)](https://www.stmarys.ac.uk/document-library/pdfs/emg-of-trunk-exercises-and-implications.pdf). Crunch end point: shoulder blades off the mat; crunch RA about 64% MVIC.
- [Snarr et al. 2016, core activation during traditional abdominal exercises (J Sport Hum Perform)](https://jhp-ojs-tamucc.tdl.org/JHP/article/view/81). Crunch vs pike, V-up, towel pike; crunch had the lowest rectus femoris activity.

### Lumbar loading

- [McGill 1995, mechanics of torso flexion: situps and standing flexion (Clin Biomech; bibliographic record)](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/485401.htm). Lumbar compression above 3,000 N in both straight-leg and bent-knee sit-ups.
- [McGill 1998, low back exercises: evidence for improving exercise regimens (Phys Ther; full-text PDF)](https://scispace.com/pdf/low-back-exercises-evidence-for-improving-exercise-regimens-42ki9u4xem.pdf). About 3,230 N straight-leg vs 3,410 N bent-knee; psoas challenge lowest in curl-ups.
- [Axler and McGill 1997, low-back loads over a variety of abdominal exercises (Med Sci Sports Exerc; bibliographic record)](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/7588015). 12 exercises; partial curl-ups had the best challenge-to-compression index.
- [Contreras and Schoenfeld 2011, to crunch or not to crunch (Strength Cond J; PDF)](https://bretcontreras.com/wp-content/uploads/To-Crunch-or-Not-to-Crunch-An-Evidence-Based-Examination-of-Spinal-Flexion-Exercises-Their-Potential-Risks-and-Their-Applicability-to-Program-Design.pdf). Narrative review of both sides; source of the 60-reps and 48-hour guidance. Opinion, not a trial.

### Training trials

- [Childs et al. 2010, sit-up training vs core stabilization and injuries in soldiers (Phys Ther; PDF)](https://academic.oup.com/ptj/article-pdf/90/10/1404/10444667/ptj1404.pdf). Cluster RCT, 1,141 soldiers; same injury rates; weak signal for fewer low-back restriction days with core work.
- [Same trial, Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1158819)
- [Childs et al. 2009, sit-up training vs core stabilization on sit-up performance (Med Sci Sports Exerc; Duke record)](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1158842). 2,616 soldiers; core training did not impair sit-up scores.

### Context (not sit-up specific)

- [Drysdale et al. 2004, abdominal EMG in pelvic tilt and hollowing (J Athl Train, PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC385259). Background on global vs deep abdominal activation.
- [Fidale et al. 2018, abdominal EMG across exercises: review protocol (Medicine, PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5944552/). Protocol only; no pooled sit-up results yet, which shows how thin the synthesis is.

## 2. Clinical and professional articles

- [Cissik, The best core exercise? (2022)](https://www.cissik.com/blog/?p=6882). Plain summary of Axler and McGill 1997 rankings.

## Gaps and limits

- No study tests a 3/4 sit-up end angle; the 60-70 degree end point comes from the GymVisual reference only.
- The "hip flexors take over past about 30 degrees" figure is a common teaching point not confirmed in any fetched source; the measured figure is lumbar flexion (about 30 degrees in a full bent-knee sit-up).
- Spine-load numbers are model estimates in small groups of young men; the injury link is extrapolated from in vitro pig spines and remains contested.
- Not verified here (pages blocked or not found): the Lee and Hodges 2016 JOSPT original, the Sinaki osteoporosis flexion studies, PubMed abstract pages, and NASM / ACE exercise-library entries for the sit-up. Web search budget ran out before these could be found by other routes.
